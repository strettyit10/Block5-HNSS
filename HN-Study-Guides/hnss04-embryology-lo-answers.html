Craniofacial Development

LO 1

Describe the anatomical structures of the tongue

Key questions

  1. Q1When does tongue development begin, and which swellings appear first?
    Weeks 4–5. Arch 1 forms two lateral lingual swellings and a midline tuberculum impar.
  2. Q2What swelling forms next, when, and from which arches?
    The copula (anterior hypobranchial eminence), weeks 5–10, from mesoderm under arches 2, 3 and 4.
  3. Q3Which swellings build the anterior 2/3 of the tongue?
    The lateral lingual swellings, which overgrow the tuberculum impar and merge.
  4. Q4What is the origin of the tongue muscle vs its epithelial lining?
    Muscle: myoblasts from the occipital somites (mesoderm). Lining: endoderm. The tongue is mesoderm surrounded by endoderm.

Anterior 2/3 comes from arch 1 (two lateral lingual swellings + tuberculum impar). Posterior 1/3 comes from the copula/hypobranchial eminence (arches 2–4). Muscles come from occipital somites.

HY · Step 1

Tongue muscles come from occipital somites, so they are innervated by CN XII (hypoglossal). The exception is palatoglossus (CN X). Tongue deviates toward a lesioned XII.

LO 2

Relate the driving mechanism of tongue development to lung development

Key questions

  1. Q5What shared developmental theme do the tongue and lung use?
    Underlying mesoderm/neural crest signals the overlying endoderm to proliferate, bud and shape the organ.
  2. Q6Which molecules carry that signal?
    Endodermal BMP-7 blocks growth; mesodermal Noggin inhibits BMP-7, releasing Patched so the endoderm proliferates. This molecular detail is lower yield.
  3. Q7How does thyroid budding differ from lung budding?
    The thyroid buds only twice (a bilobed diverticulum), while the lung keeps bifurcating.

In both, the underlying mesoderm (and neural crest) signals the overlying endoderm to proliferate: an epithelial–mesenchymal interaction.

Mesoderm + NC (mesenchyme)signals →overlying endoderm→endoderm proliferates→tongue swellings / lung buds
  • Tongue: mesoderm and NC under arches 1–4 drive the endoderm to swell into the lingual swellings and copula.
  • Lung: splanchnic mesenchyme drives the endodermal lung bud to grow and branch.
  • The thyroid also forms "much like the lungs": an endodermal diverticulum that descends as a bilobed structure. The difference: the thyroid buds only twice, while the lung keeps bifurcating.

Molecular detail: endoderm BMP-7 blocks proliferation. Mesoderm secretes Noggin, which inhibits BMP-7, releasing Patched so the endoderm proliferates. Hox proteins brought in by NC drive the mesenchyme. This molecular detail is lower yield.

LO 3

Identify the cranial innervation of the throat and tongue

Key questions

  1. Q8Which four cranial nerves innervate the tongue, and which region does each serve?
    Anterior 2/3: V3 (general sensation). Taste on the anterior 2/3: chorda tympani of VII. Posterior 1/3: IX. Extreme posterior: superior laryngeal branch of X.
  2. Q9Why do tongue regions follow those nerves?
    Each region keeps the nerve of the arch it came from: anterior from arch 1 (V3), posterior from arch 3 (IX), extreme back from arch 4 (X).
  3. Q10Which nerves carry the two limbs of the gag reflex?
    Afferent IX, efferent X.
  4. Q11A patient loses taste and sensation on the posterior 1/3 and has no gag reflex. Which nerve?
    CN IX (glossopharyngeal), the arch 3 nerve.

Tongue sensation follows its arch origin: anterior 2/3 = V3 (touch) + VII chorda tympani (taste); posterior 1/3 = IX; extreme back = X.

HY · Step 1

Bell's palsy (VII) → loss of taste on the anterior 2/3, but touch is intact (V3). A lesion of the lingual nerve before the chorda tympani joins it would lose both.

LO 4

List the steps of thyroid development

Key questions

  1. Q12Where does the thyroid start to develop?
    At the foramen cecum, between the tuberculum impar and the hypobranchial eminence.
  2. Q13What connects the thyroid to the tongue during descent, and what normally happens to it?
    The thyroglossal duct. It normally disappears.
  3. Q14Where is the thyroid at week 6, and where is it at week 7?
    Week 6: in front of the hyoid and laryngeal cartilages. Week 7: in its final position anterior to the trachea.
  4. Q15Which genes drive thyroid development?
    TTF-1 (thyroid transcription factor), PAX, BMPs and noggin. Low yield.

Starts at the foramen cecum, descends as a bilobed diverticulum on the thyroglossal duct, and reaches the front of the trachea by week 7.

  1. Endodermal epithelium proliferates at the base of the pharynx at the foramen cecum, between the tuberculum impar and hypobranchial eminence.
  2. The primordium descends from the foramen cecum as a bilobed diverticulum, like the lungs.
  3. It stays attached to the tongue by the thyroglossal duct, which later disappears.
  4. Genes: TTF-1 (thyroid transcription factor), PAX, BMPs, noggin.
  5. Week 6: descends in front of the hyoid bone and laryngeal cartilages.
  6. Week 7: reaches its final position anterior to the trachea.
  7. The ultimobranchial body (pouch 5/4) joins and gives the C cells.
HY · Step 1
  • Thyroglossal duct cyst: midline, anywhere along the descent path (often just below the hyoid), moves with swallowing/tongue protrusion. Can open as a fistula.
  • Most common ectopic site: lingual thyroid at the base of the tongue. Check for other thyroid tissue before removing it.
  • A duct remnant can persist as the pyramidal lobe.
LO 5

Identify the function of the thyroid gland during development

Key questions

  1. Q16When does the fetal thyroid begin functioning, and what does it make?
    Weeks 14–15. Follicular cells make colloid, the source of T4 and T3, which regulate cellular metabolism.
  2. Q17What do C cells secrete in the fetus, and what does it do?
    Calcitonin. It increases calcium uptake into bone and prevents resorption, which is needed for normal bone development.
  3. Q18How do fetal calcitonin levels compare with maternal levels?
    Fetal calcitonin is 2–3× higher.

Starts working at weeks 14–15: follicular cells make colloid → T4/T3 (cellular metabolism). C cells make calcitonin for normal bone development.

Follicular cells

  • Produce colloid, the source of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3)
  • Hormones control cellular metabolism
  • Levothyroxine = synthetic T4

C cells (from ultimobranchial body)

  • Secrete calcitonin (peptide hormone)
  • Binds receptors on osteocytes/osteoclasts → ↑ Ca²⁺ uptake from fetal blood → prevents bone resorption (net bone gain)
  • Fetal calcitonin is 2–3× maternal levels
HY · Step 1

Congenital hypothyroidism (cretinism): pot-bellied, pale, puffy face, protruding umbilicus, protuberant tongue, poor brain development. Newborn screening catches it; early levothyroxine prevents intellectual disability.

LO 6

Describe the anatomy of the developing face (lips, nose, cheeks) and how it forms

Key questions

  1. Q19Which five prominences surround the stomodeum at weeks 4–5?
    One frontonasal, two maxillary (lateral to the stomodeum) and two mandibular (caudal to the maxillary).
  2. Q20How do the nasal placodes turn into the medial and lateral nasal prominences?
    Weeks 5–8: the placodes invaginate into nasal pits, and the tissue ridges around each pit become the medial and lateral nasal prominences.
  3. Q21Which prominences build the upper lip?
    Two medial nasal prominences (philtrum) + two maxillary prominences. The lateral nasal prominences do not form the lip.
  4. Q22Which prominences form the bridge, the tip and the sides of the nose?
    Bridge: frontonasal. Crest and tip: merged medial nasal. Sides: lateral nasal.
  5. Q23When are the cheeks identifiable, and which prominence forms them?
    Weeks 8–9, from the maxillary prominences.
  6. Q24What tissue forms the facial bones, and what are the exceptions?
    Neural crest forms virtually all of them. Exceptions: thyroid and cricoid cartilages (and possibly the malleus and incus).

Five prominences surround the stomodeum (weeks 4–5): frontonasal, paired maxillary, paired mandibular. Between weeks 5 and 8, nasal pits split the frontonasal into medial and lateral nasal prominences, and the pieces fuse.

  • Upper lip = 2 medial nasal + 2 maxillary prominences.
  • Nose = 5 prominences: frontonasal (bridge) + 2 merged medial nasal (crest, tip) + 2 lateral nasal (sides).
  • Frontonasal prominence: mesoderm and NC inside, ectoderm outside, formed by ectoderm–mesenchyme crosstalk.
  • Intermaxillary segment (deep fusion) has 3 parts: labial → philtrum; upper jaw → 4 incisors; palatal → primary palate. It's continuous with the nasal septum.
HY · Step 1 trap

The lateral nasal prominences do not form the upper lip. Cleft lip = failure of maxillary + medial nasal fusion.

LO 7

Describe internal anatomical formation of the palates

Key questions

  1. Q25What forms the primary palate, and what are the three parts of the intermaxillary segment?
    The intermaxillary segment. Labial part → philtrum; upper jaw part → 4 incisors; palatal part → triangular primary palate.
  2. Q26What forms the secondary palate, when, and where do its parts grow?
    The palatine shelves, outgrowths of the maxillary prominences, appear at weeks 6.5–7. They grow toward each other dorsal to the tongue and fuse; the uvula hangs from the caudal end.
  3. Q27How does the nasal cavity come to open into the oral cavity?
    Week 7: the oronasal membrane separates them, then ruptures mid-week 7 to form the primitive choana, behind the primary palate.
  4. Q28How do the paranasal sinuses form, and what do they do?
    As diverticula of the lateral nasal wall into the maxilla, ethmoid, frontal and sphenoid bones. They condition inspired air and grow through puberty, shaping the face.
  5. Q29What must move out of the way for the palatine shelves to fuse?
    The tongue. The shelves grow toward each other dorsal to (above) it.

Primary palate = intermaxillary segment (medial nasal). Secondary palate = palatine shelves from the maxillary prominences, which grow over the tongue and fuse (week 6.5–7 on).

Primary palate

  • Triangular palatal component of the intermaxillary segment
  • Anterior to the incisive foramen
  • Carries the 4 incisors

Secondary (definitive) palate

  • Palatine shelves: internal outgrowths of the maxillary prominences, appear wk 6.5–7
  • Grow toward each other dorsal to the tongue and fuse
  • Fuse with the primary palate and nasal septum
  • Uvula hangs from the caudal end
Related internal structures
  • Nasal cavity: wk 6 nasal pits deepen → wk 7 oronasal membrane separates them from the mouth → mid-wk 7 it ruptures → primitive choana (behind the primary palate).
  • Paranasal sinuses: diverticula of the lateral nasal wall into the maxilla, ethmoid, frontal and sphenoid bones. They condition inspired air, keep growing through puberty and shape the face.
  • Teeth (stated in lecture as not tested): wk 6 dental lamina (C-shaped) → 10 dental buds per jaw → cap stage. Milk teeth form first; permanent buds start wk 8–15.
HY · Step 1

Cleft palate = palatine shelves fail to fuse with each other, the nasal septum and/or the primary palate. Cleft lip and cleft palate have separate embryologic causes, but often occur together.

LO 8

Describe the formation of the neurocranium and viscerocranium

Key questions

  1. Q30What is the difference between the neurocranium and the viscerocranium?
    Neurocranium surrounds the brain (cranial bones). Viscerocranium is the face and jaws. Each has a cartilaginous and a membranous part.
  2. Q31How does each one mostly ossify?
    Neurocranium: mostly endochondral, from paraxial and prechordal mesoderm plus some neural crest. Viscerocranium: mostly membranous, from neural crest plus some mesoderm.
  3. Q32What is the difference between endochondral and membranous ossification?
    Endochondral: bone replaces a pre-existing cartilage model. Membranous: mesenchyme or neural crest turns directly into bone, with no cartilage.
  4. Q33How many ossification centers and bones are involved?
    More than 100 ossification centers form 45 bones, which fuse into the 22 bones of the mature skull.

Neurocranium surrounds the brain; viscerocranium is the face and jaws. Each has a cartilaginous part (endochondral) and a membranous part (intramembranous).

LO 9

Know typical clinical malformations such as cleft lip, including their potential causes and anatomy

Key questions

  1. Q34A lateral cleft lip results from which failed fusion, and how common is it?
    Maxillary + medial nasal prominences (anterior cleft). About 1/1000 births, 80% male.
  2. Q35What fusion defect causes a cleft secondary palate and cleft uvula?
    The palatine shelves fail to fuse.
  3. Q36What causes an oblique facial cleft, and why does it come with a cleft lip?
    The maxillary prominence fails to merge with the lateral nasal prominence. The cleft lip is secondary to the cleft palate, even though the lateral nasal prominence doesn't form the lip.
  4. Q37Which drugs taken in pregnancy raise the risk of facial clefts?
    Anticonvulsants (phenobarbital, lamotrigine, valproic acid) and lithium.
  5. Q38Where along its path can a thyroglossal duct cyst be found?
    Anywhere on the thyroid's descent: below the hyoid, at the base of the tongue, or near the thyroid cartilage. If open to the skin, it is a thyroglossal fistula.
  6. Q39What is the treatment for cleft lip and cleft palate?
    Surgery.

Anterior clefts (lip) = maxillary fails to meet medial nasal. Posterior clefts (palate, uvula) = palatine shelves fail to fuse. Oblique clefts = maxillary fails to meet lateral nasal. Treatment is surgery.

HY · Step 1

Anticonvulsant teratogens: valproate (neural tube defects), phenytoin (fetal hydantoin: cleft lip/palate, hypoplastic nails), carbamazepine. Lithium's classic defect is Ebstein anomaly. Folate supplementation lowers NTD risk.

LO 10

Identify normal and potentially abnormal craniofacial development using ultrasound imaging

Key questions

  1. Q40Which structures should you identify on a normal fetal face ultrasound?
    Neurocranium, viscerocranium, frontal prominence, nose and the fused maxillary process.
  2. Q41Which two features do you check for abnormal development?
    Ear position relative to the eye and nose, and presence of the nasal bone.
  3. Q42Which fetal face finding points to trisomy 21?
    An absent nasal bone.

On a fetal face ultrasound, find the neurocranium, viscerocranium, frontal prominence, nose and fused maxillary process. The two things to check are ear position and the nasal bone.

  • Normal: identify neurocranium, viscerocranium, frontal prominence, nose, fused maxillary processes (don't confuse with hand or ribs in the image).
  • Ear position relative to the eye and nose. Low-set ears suggest an arch 1/2 or chromosomal problem.
  • Nasal bone: absent nasal bone → Down syndrome (trisomy 21) marker.
  • Facial clefts (lip, palate) can also be seen.
HY · Step 1

First-trimester Down syndrome markers: ↑ nuchal translucency and absent/hypoplastic nasal bone. Quad screen: ↓ AFP, ↑ β-hCG, ↓ estriol, ↑ inhibin A.

Application questions

1. A newborn has a cleft through the left upper lip extending into the alveolar ridge. The secondary palate is intact. Which structures failed to fuse?

  1. Palatine shelves with each other
  2. Left maxillary and medial nasal prominences
  3. Left maxillary and lateral nasal prominences
  4. The two medial nasal prominences
Answer
B. A lateral cleft lip (± primary palate) is maxillary + medial nasal. C gives an oblique facial cleft; D gives a median cleft lip.

2. A 5-year-old has a painless midline neck mass just below the hyoid that rises when he sticks out his tongue. What is its origin?

  1. Second pharyngeal cleft
  2. Third pharyngeal pouch
  3. Remnant of the thyroglossal duct
  4. Ultimobranchial body
Answer
C. Midline + moves with tongue protrusion = thyroglossal duct cyst. A lateral mass would suggest a branchial (cleft 2) cyst.
