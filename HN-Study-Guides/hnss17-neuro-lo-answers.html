HNSS17 · 7 learning objectives

Vascular & Ventricular Systems of the CNS

LO 1

Know the components of the blood brain barrier and how compounds enter the CNS from the vasculature

Key questions

  1. Q1What makes up the blood–brain barrier, and what does it block?
    Brain endothelial cells joined by tight junction proteins that mostly lack fenestrations. It blocks high-molecular-weight and charged substances.
  2. Q2What gets through the BBB?
    Small or lipophilic substances (e.g., L-dopa, estrogen) and molecules moved by specific transporters.
  3. Q3How can the BBB be useful for drug design?
    Drugs made not to cross it avoid CNS side effects — e.g., the non-drowsy antihistamine Allegra (fexofenadine).
  4. Q4How can a large biologic drug be delivered past the BBB?
    By engaging the transferrin receptor for transport by endocytosis — used for a drug for Hunter syndrome (a lysosomal storage disease of complex polysaccharides, mostly in young boys).
  5. Q5What are circumventricular organs, and which ones sense blood directly?
    Areas with an incomplete barrier. Sensing: area postrema (vomiting in response to toxins); subfornical organ and organum vasculosum (water and ion balance; immune signals → fever, warmth-seeking, loss of appetite).
  6. Q6Which circumventricular organs release substances into blood?
    Hypothalamus–pituitary (growth, homeostasis, reproduction) and the pineal gland (melatonin, circadian rhythm).

The BBB = endothelial tight junctions without fenestrations: big or charged molecules stay out; small or lipophilic ones get in. Circumventricular organs (area postrema, subfornical organ, OVLT, pituitary, pineal) are deliberate gaps for sensing blood or secreting into it.

Crosses the BBB

  • Small, lipophilic molecules (O₂, CO₂, L-dopa, estrogen)
  • Substances with transporters (glucose via GLUT1)
  • Receptor-mediated endocytosis (transferrin receptor → biologics)

Blocked

  • Large molecules (proteins)
  • Charged molecules
  • Drugs designed to stay peripheral (fexofenadine)

HY · Step 1
  • BBB = endothelial tight junctions + basement membrane + astrocyte foot processes.
  • Parkinson therapy: give L-dopa (crosses) with carbidopa (doesn't cross; blocks peripheral conversion).
  • Area postrema is the chemoreceptor trigger zone (targeted by ondansetron/D2 blockers).
  • Hyperosmolar mannitol shrinks the brain across the intact BBB; infarct, tumor and infection break it (vasogenic edema).
LO 2

Describe the mechanisms for delivery of nutrients and oxygen to the CNS

Key questions

  1. Q7How much energy does the brain use?
    20% of the body's oxygen and calories, while being only 2% of body weight.
  2. Q8How fast does lack of blood harm the brain?
    Consciousness is lost in ~10 seconds; after ~6 minutes damage is irreversible or death follows. With mild hypothermia, patients can survive 10–20 minutes with little damage.
  3. Q9How is oxygen released to active brain tissue?
    Hemoglobin releases O₂ in regions of low pH.
  4. Q10What is the brain's fuel?
    Glucose is the main ATP source; lactate and ketone bodies can supplement it.
  5. Q11How does blood flow match demand?
    Vasodilation where ATP is low (high neural activity or hypoxia); overall flow is held constant by autoregulation across a range of arterial pressures.
  6. Q12What does chronic high blood pressure do?
    Alters vessel structure → higher risk of stroke and dementia.

Brain = 2% of weight, 20% of O₂ and calories. O₂ comes off hemoglobin in low-pH tissue; glucose is the fuel (lactate, ketones backup); flow is autoregulated overall and vasodilates where activity is high.

↑ Neural activity→↓ ATP, ↓ pH, hypoxia→Local vasodilation + O₂ unloading→↑ Flow to active region

  • Autoregulation holds flow steady at MAP ~60–150 mmHg.
  • PaCO₂ is the strongest regulator: ↑ CO₂ → vasodilation → ↑ ICP; hyperventilation (↓ CO₂) → vasoconstriction → quick ↓ ICP.
  • Cushing reflex (↑ ICP): hypertension + bradycardia + irregular breathing.
  • In prolonged fasting the brain shifts to ketone bodies.
LO 3

Know the major arteries of the CNS and the structures that they perfuse

Key questions

  1. Q13Where do the internal and external carotids go?
    Common carotid → internal carotid (through the carotid canal to the Circle of Willis) and external carotid (neck, face, scalp).
  2. Q14Trace the posterior circulation.
    Vertebral arteries (branches of the subclavians) run through the transverse foramina of the cervical vertebrae, enter via the foramen magnum and join to form the basilar artery.
  3. Q15What feeds the anterior vs posterior parts of the Circle of Willis?
    Anterior: internal carotids. Posterior: vertebral (basilar) arteries.
  4. Q16Name the cerebral and cerebellar arteries.
    Cerebral: anterior (ACA), middle (MCA), posterior (PCA). Cerebellar: superior (SCA), anterior inferior (AICA), posterior inferior (PICA).
  5. Q17What supplies the pons?
    Short circumferential branches of the basilar artery — the source of pontine infarctions.
  6. Q18How is the spinal cord supplied?
    Anterior spinal artery and posterior spinal arteries branch from the vertebrals (or PICA) and run down the cord, reinforced by radicular arteries.
  7. Q19What are watershed areas?
    Border zones between the territories of two cerebral arteries, most vulnerable to low perfusion.
  8. Q20How can the vertebral arteries be damaged?
    Vertebrobasilar occlusion (atherosclerosis) or vertebral artery dissection (trauma, neck distortion).

Anterior circulation = internal carotids → ACA + MCA. Posterior circulation = vertebrals → basilar → PCA, with SCA, AICA, PICA to the cerebellum and short circumferential branches to the pons. Spinal cord = anterior + posterior spinal arteries.

HY · Step 1 (stroke syndromes)
  • MCA: contralateral face + arm weakness/sensory loss; aphasia (dominant) or neglect (nondominant). ACA: contralateral leg. PCA: contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.
  • PICA (lateral medullary, Wallenberg): vertigo, hoarseness/dysphagia (nucleus ambiguus), ipsilateral face + contralateral body pain/temp loss, ipsilateral Horner. AICA (lateral pons): facial paralysis, ipsilateral deafness.
  • Basilar occlusion → locked-in syndrome. Anterior spinal artery occlusion → loss of motor + pain/temp below lesion, dorsal columns spared.
  • Most common aneurysm site: ACom (bitemporal field loss); PCom → CN III palsy.
LO 4

Understand the various types of CNS vascular compromise

Key questions

  1. Q21Embolism vs thrombosis?
    Embolism: a traveling clot obstructs a cerebral vessel → ischemia (ischemic stroke). Thrombosis: clot forms in place, in large or small brain vessels.
  2. Q22What is an aneurysm, where does it form, and how is it treated?
    A dilation of the vessel wall, often at branch points; usually asymptomatic until it ruptures. Treated by clipping its stalk.
  3. Q23What is a hemorrhagic stroke?
    Breakage of a blood vessel with bleeding into or around the brain.
  4. Q24What is an arteriovenous malformation (AVM)?
    A tangle of abnormal vessels connecting arteries directly to veins, most common in the CNS. It reduces tissue oxygen and puts arterial pressure on weak veins/sinuses → hemorrhage. Early signs: seizures, headaches, weakness; sometimes only a pulsatile "whooshing" bruit. Treated by surgical removal.
  5. Q25What is a dural fistula?
    An AVM in which meningeal arteries connect directly to a dural sinus; years of arterial pressure raise hemorrhage risk. Treated by neuroradiology and surgery.
  6. Q26How is an intracranial hemorrhage managed?
    Control blood pressure, reduce intracranial pressure, and surgically evacuate the hematoma.
  7. Q27Which imaging shows the vessels?
    MRA and angiography show the vertebrals, basilar, internal and external carotids, Circle of Willis and cerebral arteries; CT identifies hemorrhage types.

Two big groups: ischemic (embolism, thrombosis) and hemorrhagic (vessel rupture — from aneurysm, AVM or dural fistula). Aneurysms get clipped; AVMs get removed; bleeds get BP + ICP control ± evacuation.

HY · Step 1
  • Berry aneurysm rupture → subarachnoid hemorrhage ("worst headache of my life"); risks: ADPKD, Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan, HTN, smoking. Complication: vasospasm days 3–10 (nimodipine).
  • Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms (chronic HTN) → intraparenchymal bleed in basal ganglia/thalamus.
  • Ischemic stroke: tPA within 4.5 h (after CT rules out bleed); thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion.
  • Neurons die first ~12–24 h (red neurons), then neutrophils, microglia, and a cystic cavity with gliosis.
LO 5

Know the major veins and sinuses of the CNS

Key questions

  1. Q28Name the external (superficial) veins.
    Superficial cerebral veins (superior, middle, inferior) and superficial cerebellar veins (superior, inferior).
  2. Q29Where do the internal veins drain?
    Veins of the striatum, thalamus, choroid plexus and hippocampus → internal cerebral veins → great cerebral vein (of Galen) → straight sinus.
  3. Q30What are dural venous sinuses, and how do they differ from veins?
    Spaces between the outer and inner layers of dura. No endothelial lining, no valves, so flow is not strictly one-way.
  4. Q31Name the major sinuses.
    Superior sagittal, inferior sagittal, straight, occipital, transverse, superior petrosal, inferior petrosal, sigmoid.
  5. Q32Where does all this blood end up?
    Sinuses → sigmoid sinus → internal jugular vein. Emissary veins also connect scalp veins to the sinuses.

Superficial and deep veins drain into dural venous sinuses (between the dura layers, no valves), which flow to the sigmoid sinus → internal jugular vein. Deep veins go through the great cerebral vein of Galen → straight sinus.

HY · Step 1
  • Valveless sinuses + emissary veins let scalp/face infections spread inward (danger triangle → cavernous sinus thrombosis: CN III, IV, V1, V2, VI palsies).
  • Dural venous sinus thrombosis: hypercoagulable states (pregnancy, OCPs) → headache, ↑ ICP, venous infarcts.
  • Arachnoid granulations drain CSF into the superior sagittal sinus.
LO 6

Describe the production, composition, distribution, and clearance of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)

Key questions

  1. Q33What does CSF do?
    Cushions the brain and gives buoyancy (including to the ventral arteries), removes waste, and transports hormones and ions.
  2. Q34What are the normal CSF volume, production and pressure?
    About 150 mL total; 400–500 mL made per day; normal pressure 70–180 mm H₂O.
  3. Q35Where and how is CSF made?
    By the choroid plexus — tufts of specialized ependymal cells folded into villi in the lateral, third and fourth ventricles — which filters arterial blood and actively transports some molecules (choroidal artery and vein).
  4. Q36Compare CSF with plasma.
    CSF: K⁺ 2.8 mM (plasma 4.5), Ca²⁺ 1.0 mM (2.4), protein 34 mg/dL (6500), 0–5 WBCs and no RBCs.
  5. Q37How does CSF flow and leave?
    Choroid plexus → through the ventricles → into the subarachnoid space around brain and cord → through arachnoid granulations into the superior sagittal sinus.
  6. Q38What are arachnoid granulations, and why is flow one-way?
    Protrusions of arachnoid through the dura, only in the superior sagittal sinus. CSF pressure exceeds venous pressure, so CSF flows one way into the sinus.
  7. Q39What CSF findings point to which disease?
    Neutrophils + cloudy: bacterial meningitis. Lymphocytes (IgG): active multiple sclerosis. RBCs: subarachnoid hemorrhage. Malignant cells: metastatic cancer.
  8. Q40When is a lumbar puncture justified?
    Signs such as severe headache, stiff neck, confusion, seizures, persistent flu-like symptoms, fatigue, altered consciousness, severe nausea, fever or rash, light sensitivity, poor coordination.

Choroid plexus makes ~500 mL/day; ~150 mL circulates through ventricles → subarachnoid space → arachnoid granulations → superior sagittal sinus. CSF has less K⁺, Ca²⁺ and far less protein than plasma, and almost no cells.

  • Meningitis CSF: bacterial ↑ PMNs, ↑ protein, ↓ glucose; viral ↑ lymphocytes, normal glucose; fungal/TB ↑ lymphocytes, ↓ glucose, ↑↑ protein.
  • MS: oligoclonal IgG bands. SAH: xanthochromia (yellow) after hours.
  • LP is done at L3–L4 or L4–L5 (below the conus at L1–L2); rule out mass effect first to avoid herniation.
LO 7

Understand the symptoms and treatment of the different types of hydrocephalus

Key questions

  1. Q41What is hydrocephalus, and what causes it?
    Increased CSF volume and pressure with enlarged ventricles, from blocked CSF movement or failed absorption.
  2. Q42What is obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus?
    A block inside the ventricular system — e.g., at the interventricular foramen or cerebral aqueduct.
  3. Q43What is communicating hydrocephalus, and what causes it?
    CSF flows freely through the ventricles and subarachnoid space, but can't get into the venous system: congenital absence of arachnoid villi, or villi blocked by RBCs after hemorrhage or protein after infection.
  4. Q44What is normal pressure hydrocephalus?
    A misnomer — CSF pressure is elevated across repeated measurements. Usually elderly. Triad: urinary problems, dementia, impaired gait ("wet, wacky and wobbly").
  5. Q45How is hydrocephalus treated?
    Shunting, removing the blockage, or supportive care.
  6. Q46How does a CSF shunt work?
    It drains CSF from a ventricle when pressure rises, through subcutaneous tubing, usually to the peritoneum. About a 1-hour surgery, adjustable to CSF pressure, 50–80% success.

Obstructive = block inside the ventricles (foramen, aqueduct). Communicating = flow OK but absorption at arachnoid villi fails (hemorrhage, infection, absent villi). NPH = elderly, wet, wacky, wobbly. Treat with shunt (ventriculoperitoneal), removal of the block, or support.

Memory hook

NPH = wet (urinary incontinence), wacky (dementia), wobbly (gait) — and it's reversible with a shunt, unlike Alzheimer's.

HY · Step 1
  • NPH: gait improves after large-volume lumbar tap → predicts shunt response. Stretching of periventricular corona radiata fibers causes the triad.
  • Hydrocephalus ex vacuo: ventricles look big because brain has atrophied (Alzheimer, HIV) — pressure normal, not true hydrocephalus.
  • Congenital obstructive causes: aqueductal stenosis, Dandy-Walker (4th ventricle cyst), Chiari II.
  • Shunt complications: infection, obstruction, over-drainage.

Application questions

1. A 72-year-old has a shuffling, "magnetic" gait, urinary incontinence and forgetfulness. MRI shows enlarged ventricles out of proportion to sulci. What is the diagnosis, and what treatment may reverse it?

  1. Alzheimer disease — cholinesterase inhibitor
  2. Normal pressure hydrocephalus — ventriculoperitoneal shunt
  3. Parkinson disease — levodopa
  4. Hydrocephalus ex vacuo — none
  5. Communicating hydrocephalus from meningitis — antibiotics
Answer
B. Wet, wacky and wobbly with ventriculomegaly = NPH; a shunt can reverse it.

2. A patient has vertigo, hoarseness, trouble swallowing, loss of pain and temperature on the left face and right body, and a left Horner syndrome. Which artery is occluded?

  1. Left MCA
  2. Left PICA
  3. Basilar artery
  4. Right ACA
  5. Anterior spinal artery
Answer
B. Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome from PICA (or vertebral) occlusion; ipsilateral signs are on the left.

3. CSF from a febrile patient with neck stiffness is cloudy with many neutrophils. What is the diagnosis?

  1. Multiple sclerosis
  2. Subarachnoid hemorrhage
  3. Bacterial meningitis
  4. Metastatic cancer
  5. Normal CSF
Answer
C. Neutrophil-predominant, cloudy CSF indicates bacterial meningitis; lymphocytes/IgG suggest MS; RBCs suggest SAH.

4. A new antihistamine is designed so it does not cause drowsiness. Which property keeps it out of the brain?

  1. Small size
  2. High lipophilicity
  3. Large or charged structure that can't cross endothelial tight junctions
  4. Transport by GLUT1
  5. Entry via the transferrin receptor
Answer
C. The BBB blocks large and charged molecules; non-sedating antihistamines like fexofenadine are designed to stay peripheral.

5. After a subarachnoid hemorrhage, a patient develops ventricular enlargement with open ventricular pathways. What is the mechanism?

  1. Aqueductal stenosis
  2. Blood cells clogging the arachnoid granulations
  3. Overproduction by a choroid plexus tumor
  4. Brain atrophy
  5. Absent choroid plexus
Answer
B. Communicating hydrocephalus: RBCs block absorption at the arachnoid villi even though flow through the ventricles is open.
