HNSS08 · 3 learning objectives

Brain Development

LO 1

Know the stages of brain development throughout life to know their order and possible failures

Key questions

  1. Q1List the stages of brain development in order.
    Neural induction → neurulation → formation of brain divisions (vesicles) → neuronal proliferation and migration → circuit formation (axon generation and guidance) → neuronal competition → synaptogenesis → myelination → synaptic pruning → gray and white matter expansion → changes in healthy aging.
  2. Q2What is neural induction, and what happens if it fails?
    Ectoderm cells differentiate to a neural fate because of external molecular signals/gradients (blocking BMP). Induction also means loss of potential. Failure → natural (spontaneous) abortion.
  3. Q3What is neurulation, and when does it occur?
    The neural plate folds to form the neural tube, in weeks 3–4 of gestation. (Gestational age counts from the last menstrual period, ~2 weeks before fertilization.)
  4. Q4Name the three signaling sources that pattern the neural tube.
    Roof plate, floor plate and somites. Floor plate → SHH; roof plate → BMPs (and retinoic acid); somites → BMPs.
  5. Q5What is the five-vesicle stage?
    By gestational week 6: telencephalon, diencephalon, mesencephalon, metencephalon, myelencephalon — with folds, axes, differential growth and the optic vesicles.
  6. Q6Where are cortical neurons born, and how do progenitors divide?
    In the ventricular zone. Symmetric division → two progenitors; asymmetric division → one progenitor + one non-dividing neuron.
  7. Q7How do cortical neurons migrate, and in what order are layers built?
    Excitatory neurons climb radial glia toward the marginal zone (inhibitory neurons migrate tangentially). Layers form inside-out: deepest first, later neurons pass them. Cortical development spans gestational weeks 7–18. The neocortex has 6 layers.
  8. Q8How do axons find their targets?
    A motile growth cone senses its environment and follows diffuse gradients (Eph receptors and their ephrin ligands) and non-diffuse extracellular signals.
  9. Q9What is the neurotrophic factor hypothesis?
    Target cells make limited neurotrophic factor; neurons that don't get enough undergo apoptosis. Factors: neurotrophins (NGF, BDNF, NT-3, NT-4/5), BMPs, hedgehogs, TGF-β, interleukins.
  10. Q10Why does the cortex grow after birth?
    Mainly dendritic growth, spine formation and synaptogenesis — not new neurons. Synapse changes continue for life.
  11. Q11What drives myelination, and what does it do?
    Oligodendrocyte development interacting with signals from individual axons; myelin greatly speeds conduction. Myelination reflects stabilization of projections.
  12. Q12Give the cortical maturation timeline.
    Rapid volume growth in the first 3 years, then leveling off and decline. Age 4: primary sensory, visual and motor cortex mature. Age 6: language early, prefrontal immature. Age 9: parietal and basal ganglia maturing. Age 11: limbic, parietal, prefrontal increasing. Age 15–18: association cortex matures by pruning. Age 21: most regions adult, but prefrontal and superior temporal still maturing.
  13. Q13What changes in healthy aging?
    Gray matter loss (larger ventricles and sulci) and lower synaptic density; some cognitive and sensory processes decline. Only some people develop severe degeneration (Alzheimer, other dementias, vascular).

Induction → neurulation (wk 3–4) → vesicles (5 by wk 6) → proliferation + migration (wk 7–18, inside-out) → axon guidance → competition → synaptogenesis → myelination → pruning → expansion → aging. Earlier failures are more severe.

HY · Step 1
  • Neural tube closes by ~day 28 (post-fertilization): anterior neuropore ~day 25 (anencephaly), posterior ~day 27 (spina bifida).
  • Neural tube defects → ↑ AFP in maternal serum and amniotic fluid; ↑ acetylcholinesterase in amniotic fluid.
  • Neural crest vs neural tube: tube → CNS neurons, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, ependyma; crest → PNS (DRG, autonomic ganglia, Schwann cells), melanocytes, adrenal medulla.
LO 2

Understand the role of environmental cues in brain development to understand teratogens and genetic malfunctions

Key questions

  1. Q14What is a teratogen?
    An agent that causes abnormality after fetal exposure during pregnancy.
  2. Q15Which environmental factors raise neural tube defect risk?
    Folate (folic acid) deficiency, maternal insulin-dependent diabetes and certain maternal anticonvulsants. About 3,000 US pregnancies a year are affected; rates are higher in Hispanic women.
  3. Q16Which genes cause lissencephaly, and what happens?
    Recessive Reelin mutations and doublecortin (DCX) mutations disrupt neuronal migration → smooth brain (lissencephaly) or laminar heterotopia. Outcomes: abortion, seizures, early death; severe cases live ~10 years.
  4. Q17Which gene is linked to agenesis of the corpus callosum?
    DCC (an axon-guidance receptor). Often diagnosed in the first 2 years; may cause intellectual disability, subtler psychosocial problems, or nothing. A seizure may be the first sign.
  5. Q18How do Fragile X and Rett differ at the synapse?
    Fragile X: increased dendritic spine density. Rett: decreased spine density. Subnormal intellect tracks spine morphology.
  6. Q19Why can one signaling defect cause broad abnormalities?
    The same signaling molecules (e.g., BMPs) are reused at many stages and in many systems.
  7. Q20Why does the timing of exposure matter?
    The time of interference determines which developing function is hit — e.g., fetal alcohol effects differ with when in pregnancy drinking occurred.
  8. Q21What is the most common infectious cause of birth defects in the US, and its effects?
    Cytomegalovirus (CMV). ~1 in 200 babies are born with it (higher in African Americans); 1 in 5 of those have symptoms: hearing loss, developmental and motor delay, microcephaly. Rubella and Zika also affect brain development.

Development runs on location-based signals; genes (Reelin, DCX, DCC) or teratogens (folate lack, diabetes, anticonvulsants, alcohol, CMV) that disturb them cause defects, and when the hit happens decides what breaks.

HY · Step 1
  • Folic acid 0.4 mg/day before conception (4 mg if prior NTD); valproate and carbamazepine → NTDs.
  • Congenital CMV: periventricular calcifications, SNHL, microcephaly, chorioretinitis. Toxoplasma: diffuse calcifications, hydrocephalus. Zika: severe microcephaly.

  • Isotretinoin (retinoic acid) and maternal diabetes (caudal regression) are classic CNS teratogens.
LO 3

Describe the outcomes of deficits in different mechanisms and at brain development stages

Key questions

  1. Q22What is the outcome of failed neural induction?
    Natural (spontaneous) abortion.
  2. Q23What is the outcome of failed neurulation?
    Neural tube defects — e.g., spina bifida / myelomeningocele (caudal) or anencephaly (cranial).
  3. Q24What is the outcome of failed neuronal migration?
    Lissencephaly (smooth brain) or laminar heterotopia: seizures, intellectual disability, early death.
  4. Q25What is the outcome of failed axon guidance of crossing fibers?
    Agenesis of the corpus callosum: from no symptoms to seizures and intellectual disability.
  5. Q26What is the outcome of abnormal synaptogenesis / spine regulation?
    Intellectual disability and autism-spectrum features — Fragile X (too many spines), Rett (too few).
  6. Q27Why are earlier failures usually worse?
    Early stages build the foundation every later stage depends on, so failures cascade; later failures hit narrower functions.

The earlier the failure, the worse the outcome: induction → abortion; neurulation → NTDs; migration → lissencephaly; guidance → callosal agenesis; synaptogenesis → Fragile X / Rett.

HY · Step 1
  • Spina bifida occulta: tuft of hair/dimple, normal AFP. Meningocele: meninges herniate. Myelomeningocele: meninges + cord herniate; linked to Chiari II and hydrocephalus.
  • Holoprosencephaly: failed forebrain splitting (SHH mutation, trisomy 13, FAS) → cyclopia, cleft lip.

Application questions

1. A fetal MRI at 24 weeks shows a smooth cerebral surface with no gyri. Which process failed, and which genes are implicated?

  1. Neural induction — BMP
  2. Neuronal migration — Reelin or DCX
  3. Myelination — oligodendrocyte genes
  4. Axon guidance — DCC
  5. Synaptic pruning — FMR1
Answer
B. Lissencephaly is a migration defect caused by Reelin (recessive) or doublecortin (DCX) mutations.

2. A 1-year-old's first seizure leads to MRI showing no fibers crossing between the hemispheres. Which gene was named in lecture?

  1. DCX
  2. Reelin
  3. DCC
  4. SHH
  5. PAX6
Answer
C. DCC mutations disrupt axon guidance of crossing fibers → agenesis of the corpus callosum; a seizure can be the first sign.

3. A newborn fails the hearing screen and has microcephaly. The mother had a mild flu-like illness in pregnancy. Most likely cause?

  1. Rubella
  2. Cytomegalovirus
  3. Toxoplasma
  4. Herpes simplex
  5. Syphilis
Answer
B. CMV is the most common infectious cause of birth defects in the US; hearing loss and microcephaly are typical.

4. Why does the prefrontal cortex remain vulnerable to environment into the early 20s?

  1. It is the first region to myelinate
  2. It is still maturing (pruning and myelination) past age 21
  3. It has no synapses until puberty
  4. Neurogenesis continues there
  5. It lacks radial glia
Answer
B. Prefrontal and superior temporal cortex are still maturing at 21; pruning and myelination continue there.

5. A girl develops normally for 18 months, then loses language and develops hand-wringing. Her dendritic spines are most likely:

  1. Increased in density
  2. Decreased in density
  3. Normal
  4. Absent entirely
  5. Replaced by axons
Answer
B. Rett syndrome is associated with decreased spine density; Fragile X shows increased density.
